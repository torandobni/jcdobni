Gunner John Charles Dobni

1922 – 1980 L-57721 Royal Canadian Artillery Canadian Army (Active) 1941–1945 Saskatoon Light Infantry 1954–1958

A Kindersley farm boy who enlisted at 18, crossed the Atlantic, served in six countries and came home to build a business in the town he grew up in.

For the Kindersley Remembrance Day supplement

His service

Gunner John Charles Dobni (L-57721) was born in Kindersley on 4 July 1922 to William and Annie Dobni. He was farming and driving trucks and tractors when he enlisted in the Royal Canadian Artillery at Rosetown on 24 June 1941, a week before his 19th birthday. From his first pay he sent part of his wages home to his mother, $10 a month and then $20, and he did not miss a payment for the rest of the war.

He reported to Camp Petawawa, Ontario, on 19 August 1941 to train with the 67th Light Anti-Aircraft Battery, 7th Light Anti-Aircraft Regiment. He sailed from Halifax on 12 November 1941 and landed at Gourock, Scotland, on 22 November. For the next two and a half years he served with the regiment on anti-aircraft duty in England, around Colchester and later, it appears, near Brighton. He qualified as a Regimental Signaller in January 1943 and as a Driver Operator that July, the trade he held for the rest of the war.

In late March 1944 he sailed for the Mediterranean, landing in Italy on 9 April. He served first as a Driver Operator at the headquarters of the 1st Canadian Army Group, Royal Canadian Artillery, during the spring offensive in the Liri Valley. In mid-June he joined the 4th Canadian Anti-Tank Regiment, 5th Canadian Armoured Division, at Alvignano, near Caserta in south-western Italy, where the division was refitting. In August the regiment moved north to the Adriatic front for the assault on the Gothic Line. In September it fought at Coriano Ridge and on toward Rimini. It spent the winter in the river battles of the Romagna, around Cervia and Ravenna.

In February 1945 the whole of the 1st Canadian Corps was moved in secret from Italy to North-West Europe. John landed in France on 26 February and was in Belgium by March. In April he took part in the liberation of the Netherlands, advancing into Friesland and ending the war in Groningen, where he stayed with the occupation force until the autumn.

His record shows a steady, dependable soldier. He earned the Good Conduct Badge in 1943, and in more than four years his file shows no lost or forfeited service. He also stayed remarkably healthy. His only recorded illness was a throat infection in 1943, and his discharge medical rated him fit in every category, 12 pounds heavier than on the day he enlisted.

He was honourably discharged at Regina on 20 November 1945 after 53 months in uniform, 48 of them overseas in the U.K., Italy, France, Belgium, Holland and Germany. He was awarded the 1939–45 Star, Italy Star, France and Germany Star, Defence Medal, Canadian Volunteer Service Medal with Clasp and War Medal 1939–45.

When he left the army his plans were modest: farming, and part-time work in a beer parlour. Instead he became a businessman. He used his veterans' credit to equip his own business in 1946 and later owned and ran a movie theatre in Kindersley. In 1954 he enlisted again, serving part-time with the Saskatoon Light Infantry until 1958 and rising to corporal.

This Remembrance Day, Kindersley remembers Gunner John Charles Dobni, one of its own, who left the farm at 18 to stand against the Axis forces in Europe, fought across six countries to defend our freedoms, and came home to become one of the town's successful entrepreneurs.

The Grand Hotel in 1944

A Salvation Army leave centre

The Grand Hotel, "a first-class resort in peacetime", opened in 1944 as a Salvation Army leave centre for Canadian troops serving in Italy. It was for non-commissioned officers and men, and could take 500 at a time "in rooms with clean sheets and hot water." The army supplied the food; Italian chefs and waiters cooked and served it. "There were movies, army shows and dance music every night." Everyone in 1st Canadian Corps was eligible for a 48-hour pass.

One of the Salvation Army officers who ran it was Paul Willison, who managed Red Shield leave centres for Canadians in Italy, including the Grand Hotel, the Beaver Club in Campobasso and the Monastery Inn in Ortona. He was made an MBE for the work.

For the men who came out of the Gothic Line and Coriano, historian Terry Copp writes, the war for a brief time "seemed to exist in a parallel universe." Outside, it was a season of cold October rain: rivers in flood, bridges that could not be built, and on 20–23 October the fighting on the Savio.

Sources: Canadian War Museum, The Salvation Army exhibition, panel 13; Terry Copp, "Cold October Rain", Legion Magazine, 2010; "Living History: Giving Hope Yesterday", Salvationist, 2024.

Marks noted at enlistment: a vaccination scar on the left arm and small brown moles on both arms. His recruit medical also recorded an old fracture of the right collarbone and moderately flat feet. Neither held him back.

Health

One illness in four and a half years: a throat infection that put him in the 22nd Canadian Field Ambulance from 2 to 3 July 1943 in England. He was back on duty the next day.

His eyes were the one thing that changed. He tested 20/40 at enlistment and was fitted for army spectacles in October 1942. At his discharge board he said, in his own words: "I need a pair of glasses, otherwise I have no complaints." The doctor added that his eyes "seem to be sore all the time", worse with reading, and recommended a refraction before he left the army.

The board's verdict on everything else: "No illness in Army." "No treatment required currently." PULHEMS 1 in every category.

Army dentists saw him at Rosetown in August 1941, in the field in June 1943, at 2 C.A.R.U. just before he sailed for Italy in March 1944, with the 4th Anti-Tank Regiment in January 1945, and on discharge day, when all he needed was a cleaning.

Conduct

Awarded the Good Conduct Badge effective 24 June 1943, his second anniversary in uniform.

The war service gratuity computation lists non-qualifying service, forfeitures and overpayments. Every box reads Nil.

His discharge was honourable, "to return to civil life (on demobilization)".

5th Canadian Armoured Division · 1st Canadian Corps

The Mighty Maroon Machine

The division's maroon shoulder patch gave it its nickname. It was Canada's second armoured division, sent to Italy at the end of 1943.

The 4th Anti-Tank Regiment, Royal Canadian Artillery, was the division's own anti-tank artillery. Its batteries were usually split up and attached to the infantry and armoured brigades in action, to hold ground against counter-attacks by German tanks. As a Driver Operator, John's job was to drive the regiment's vehicles and work its wireless sets, the link between the guns and their headquarters. When he was discharged the army rated the civilian equivalent of his trade as "Land Station Operator", a radio operator.

He joined the regiment in June 1944, just after the division's first big battle in the Liri Valley, and stayed with it through every major action that followed, in two countries, until August 1945.

Before that, from late April 1944, he was a Driver Operator at the headquarters of the 1st Canadian Army Group, Royal Canadian Artillery, which controlled the corps' medium and heavy guns during the May offensive.

After the war

He told the army he planned to go back to farming and work part-time in a beer parlour. The file shows what he did instead.

1946

On 17 May, Veterans Affairs approved $757.50 of his re-establishment credit for "Equipment for his business." The cheque was made out to him and Howard's Garage, Kindersley.

1950s

Owner of a movie theatre in Kindersley. His civilian trade on the 1954 record card: "movie picture operator."

1954

Enrolled again on 24 January with the Saskatoon Light Infantry (MG), new number L-454765. Former service noted: "1941–45 (4 Anti Tank 5th Div)."

1955

Promoted corporal on 1 May. Completed the Junior NCOs course on 5 July with the 2nd Battalion, North Saskatchewan Regiment.

1957–58

Re-engaged for two more years on 24 January 1957. Released at Kindersley on 15 March 1958.

1980

Died in April 1980, aged 57.

Brighton is inferred from the leaflet and programme he kept (September 1942 and December 1943); the regiment's 1942–43 war diaries could confirm it. Timeline entries in Britain without a known place are shown on the map without a pin.
  • The service file names Gourock but not his port of arrival in Italy. The map places that landing at Naples, the usual reinforcement port, as an approximation.
